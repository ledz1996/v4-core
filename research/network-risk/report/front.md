# PoolManager network risk report

State on 2026-11-24: cycles 1 to 9 are merged. The ledger holds 116 findings, 1,024 checks that came out safe, and 479 open leads across the 21 networks. About 160 agents have worked on it so far, roughly half of them as independent verifiers.

## Opcodes and the pool math

On 24 November the review turned to one narrower question: does any network compute an opcode, or run control flow, differently from Ethereum in a way that changes the PoolManager's arithmetic or logic? The full account is in `OPCODES.md`. No difference was found.

- 185,529 opcode test vectors gave the specified result on all 21 networks. They include 8,908 inputs built to hit a wrong `MULMOD` result that one release of the integer library under reth based nodes shipped in October 2026.
- The deployed PoolManager bytecode, placed with the same empty state on every network, answered 550 scripts of swaps, liquidity changes, hook callbacks and token transfers byte for byte as on Ethereum in 10,985 of 11,000 comparisons. The other 15 stop at a swap that ran out of gas on one side.
- 99,903 mined swaps satisfy the tick relation of `TickMath`, and 85,278 match the single-step formulas of `SwapMath` to the unit. Of 229 multi-step swaps that were replayed step by step from chain storage, 228 match to the unit and one could not be read.
- The node source of Monad, BNB Smart Chain, Polygon, Avalanche, Tempo, MegaETH and Arc shows no opcode that departs from the specification. BNB Smart Chain carried a bytecode fusion feature with real faults until v1.7.8 and removed it in v1.8.0.

- This checkout builds to a PoolManager of 24,009 bytes whose creation code hash equals the one in `snapshots/PoolManagerTest.json`. A reference interpreter written for the review, with no client code in it, gives the same answer as each of the 21 networks on 1,287 scripts that execute 95.6 percent of the built bytecode, apart from scripts a node could not afford in gas. Its step trace equals Tempo's over 2.9 million steps, and on 4,000 random pools the build's swap and liquidity amounts equal the source's formulas.

The faulty library release (ruint 1.21.0) runs on none of the 21 networks. Ethereum's reth nodes and Tempo run the first fixed release, which is a month old, and Tempo has no second client. As a by-product of running both, the built bytecode turned out to be the same bytes as the PoolManager deployed on Ethereum, so the on-chain measurements apply to this build directly.

## The question

Can an ordinary account take funds that other users hold in this repository's PoolManager, or stop them from withdrawing, on any of the 21 networks where Uniswap v4 is officially deployed? The attacker has no role: no owner or fee controller key, no token admin key, no sequencer or validator power, no governance vote. The victims are liquidity providers, swappers and holders of ERC-6909 claims who use pools in the normal way.

## Answer so far

No critical or high finding exists for an ordinary attacker, and the planted weakness has not been identified. Nine cycles have found no property of any of the 21 networks that lets an ordinary account reach funds in pools of ordinary tokens. Every finding that an ordinary account can trigger depends on a particular token or a particular third-party hook, and the amounts at stake today are small.

Cycle 7 went back to the OP Stack chains, Tempo and Arc, HyperEVM, Monad and MegaETH, walked the five audit reports in `docs/security/audits` against `src/` finding by finding, and swept every priced currency for transfers that cost the sender more than the amount. The network units found nothing an ordinary account can use. The audit walk found every reported hazard closed in the source or left open by a recorded decision of the team, and two guards that the reports list as fixes and the source does not have, neither of which moves funds. The sweep raised the amount behind one earlier finding: the Arc PoolManager holds about 107,000 USD of a token that charges the sender on top.

Fifty-four findings can be triggered by an ordinary account: 16 medium, 32 low and 6 that only record a mismatch between the source and its comments, tests or audit record. They fall into ten groups.

Capture of balance growth (F-041, F-066, F-067, F-091 medium; F-060, F-081, F-110 low). Any account can call `sync`, cause the PoolManager's balance of a token to rise, and call `settle` to be credited the rise. For Ampleforth on Ethereum the public rebase is guarded by `msg.sender == tx.origin`, and an EIP-7702 delegated account passes that check while running code. On Polygon the capture is in regular use: since 22 May 2026 six accounts have wrapped each sLGNS rebase in `sync` and `settle` and minted the PoolManager's share as claims, 661 mints for 20,418.69 sLGNS, about 3,300 USD. The same is done with sSAC on BNB Smart Chain, where F-066 had recorded the path as unused: a cycle 6 verifier counted 188 payout transactions for 271.92 sSAC since block 115,890,216. Smaller cases are Interest Protocol's USDI on Ethereum, Benqi rewards on qiAVAX on Avalanche, TITHE on Robinhood Chain, where a public `claimFor` call pays 10,002.56 TITHE of holder rewards to the PoolManager, and a MortgageFi vault share on Base, where the same kind of call adds 2.58 shares. In every case the amount taken is growth that no pool, position or claim was credited with, and principal does not fall. A search of 53.3 million claim mint logs on the 21 networks found mints without a matching payment only on Polygon, in three ids that were already known.

Tokens that let a third party lock or remove the PoolManager's balance (F-078, F-079, F-085, F-090 medium; F-082, F-095, F-099 low). These reach funds that pools have recorded, which the growth captures above do not.

- Two QANX contracts and seventeen tokens from one generator count locked units in `balanceOf` and let any holder send locked units to another address. An account can take liquid units from the PoolManager, pay back in locked ones between `sync` and `settle`, and leave the pools with units that cannot be sent. A holder of locked QANX gains the liquid units. This works on Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain and Polygon. About 133 USD of current QANX and about 250 USD of generator tokens are exposed, and the 21.2 million old QANX in the Ethereum PoolManager belong to a contract its issuer abandoned in 2022.
- Old ADX and CDT on Ethereum let any holder grant revocable vesting tokens. An account can take X, grant X back to the PoolManager, settle, and then revoke the grant, which removes X with no debit. The verifier reproduced it with `eth_simulateV1`. The PoolManager holds 16.6 ADX and 310 CDT, without a market price.
- DeadToken on Base lets a holder of 1,000,000 DEAD claim the balance of any address that has been idle for 90 days. On 17 November 2026 an ordinary account borrowed that amount and took the PoolManager's whole balance, 14,899.78 DEAD worth about 4 USD, with no PoolManager call. The pools still record it.
- XAMP has a public negative rebase, and 37 unpriced tokens have burn or transfer functions without a caller check. The same call empties any other holder of these tokens.
- Alpaca ibBUSD on BNB Smart Chain has a public `claimFor(address)` that converts a holder's shares into a newer vault token. Called for the PoolManager, it burns the whole pooled balance, 56.66 ibBUSD worth about 56 USD, and stakes the replacement in a contract the PoolManager cannot call. Nobody has sent the call yet. The 16 pools carry fees of 88 to 99.9996 percent, so they may be bait pools.
- Imperium on Polygon lets any account burn the balance of an address that has received nothing for 90 days, and the PoolManager qualifies today. The only liquidity provider of the two pools is the token owner, so no third party funds are at risk. BankToken on Ethereum and a DeadToken copy on Polygon carry the same kind of rule over dust.
- FACTR and TC on BNB Smart Chain join the QANX group. The verifier corrected one point for the whole group: the caller needs no tokens of its own, because the take supplies them, and the effect is a freeze of the pooled units.

The verifier rated the first two high as a class. The review files them as medium, in line with F-041, because the loss is limited to pools of the named tokens. A new deployment has the same exposure once someone creates a pool in such a token and others add liquidity to it. The search behind them ran 389,956 read-only trials over 1,235,609 pooled currencies and found such tokens on these three networks only. Cycle 6 then tested 2,151 priced tokens on 19 networks with every selector in their code, at four block times, and found no token worth more than 1,000 USD at a PoolManager that lets a stranger lower or lock the balance, apart from QANX. That test could not see a charge on an ordinary transfer, which is the next group.

A token that charges the sender on top of the amount (F-092, F-109, medium, Ethereum and Arc). `take` debits the caller by the amount and assumes the token lowers the PoolManager's balance by the same amount. Two priced tokens break that when the recipient is their listed exchange pool. WHITE on Ethereum takes 10 percent from the sender on top, so a `take` of X to its Uniswap v2 pair lowers the PoolManager's balance by 1.1 X. A loop that costs only gas (take to the pair, skim the tokens back to the PoolManager, settle) moves 10 percent of the amount from the pooled balance to the token owner each round and can empty it. The caller gains nothing unless it is the token owner. The PoolManager holds 48.3 million WHITE, about 1,460 USD. ARGUS on Arc charges 1 percent the same way, and the Arc PoolManager holds 46.6 million ARGUS, about 107,000 USD. A seller that routes its sale through `take` leaves the charge to the liquidity providers of the v4 pools, which already record about 188,000 ARGUS more than the PoolManager holds. The deliberate round trip is not free on Arc, because tokens coming back from the pool arrive at 99 percent. The cycle 7 sweep sent a third of the PoolManager's balance of 13,001 priced currencies to 128,687 candidate recipients on the 21 networks, and these two are the only ones above rounding. The cause is the token in both cases.

A transfer cap that one account can use up (F-072, F-083, medium, Polygon). ViciCoin limits every address that its owner has not exempted to 6 VCNT of net outflow per UTC day, and the PoolManager is not exempt. An account that withdraws 6 VCNT at the start of a day makes every later VCNT withdrawal from every v4 pool revert until someone pays VCNT in. Cycle 5 added the consequence for positions. The PositionManager on all 21 networks rejects the `MINT_6909` action, so a removal built by the published SDK reverts as a whole when one currency cannot leave, and the other currency of the position stays locked with it. A hand-built exit that places the blocked side in a new one-sided position works. Of the 15 ViciCoin pools, 8 have a position held through the PositionManager, and the PoolManager holds about 206 USD of VCNT. On the other 20 networks the same lock needs a token issuer to block the PoolManager first.

A ledger shared between pools of one hook (F-084, F-096 medium; F-097, F-098 low). The Klik fee hooks on nine networks keep one pending fee amount per launched token and burn it in the currency of whichever pool of that token swaps next. They accept a pool of the token against any currency from any account. An account that opens a second pool against a currency of its choice can make the hook burn claims that back other tokens' fees. This has happened: an Ethereum transaction burned about 11 USD of claims in a tokenised stock currency, and three pools with liquidity now revert on every swap. Cycle 6 found the gain behind it. The Klik factories record creator fees without a currency and pay them in the token's listed pair, so fees credited in a worthless currency are paid out in the real one. One account used this in four Ethereum transactions and took about 130 USD of other creators' fees. Three route hooks on Arbitrum One pay their exchange from their own token balance whatever currencies the pool names, which puts about 124 USD of the operators' money within reach of any account, and SpawnerHook on Base pays one shared ETH fee bucket to the creator of whichever token is sold next. In all of these the funds sit in the hook or its factory, liquidity providers can still withdraw, and the PoolManager behaves as written. The hook census probed 111 hooks on the 21 networks in cycle 5 and the 20 largest holders per network in cycle 6. The other shared hooks it read, including FeeHookV3, Clanker and Doppler, reject a pool from an ordinary account or keep their ledgers per pool.

Yield that nobody can withdraw (F-012 stETH on Ethereum, medium; F-037 Aave aTokens on Arbitrum One and F-070 receipt tokens on thirteen networks, low). The balance grows outside pool accounting and core has no function that pays the growth out. Principal is untouched.

Payouts to a recipient that swallows them (F-033 medium; F-021, F-047, F-048, F-049 low). On every network family some address accepts a payout from the PoolManager and books it to the PoolManager itself, or holds it: HyperCore bridge addresses on HyperEVM, `L2ToL1MessagePasser` and the ERC-4337 EntryPoints on the OP Stack chains, withdrawal predeploys and fee vaults on Celo and X Layer, precompiles and custody addresses on Arc and Tempo, and Tempo accounts whose receive policy rejects the PoolManager. The caller is debited in full each time, so the only loser is the caller, or the user of a router that passes a recipient through unchecked.

Currency ids that take credit and cannot pay out (F-008, F-077, F-050, F-025, low). Polygon's system token at `0x1010`, the USDC implementation address on Arc, and the Celo predeploy that names itself WETH and wraps CELO. Only someone who pays into such an id loses. One empty pool with `0x1010` as a currency now exists on Polygon.

Delay without loss (F-038, F-039, F-042, F-044, F-062, F-063, F-064, F-071, F-106, low). One cheap transaction on MegaETH uses a block's whole budget for new state. A HyperEVM small block fits about 200 tick crossings in one swap. A HyperCore transfer runs a linked contract ahead of every signed transaction in the block. Any account can fill one tick to the liquidity cap for dust, on every network. On Tempo a first crossing of a tick with a dust position costs about 505,000 gas. In a transaction that several users share, a `sync` left by an earlier user makes a later user's native payment revert if that user's router sends value without its own `sync`. The AGL hook on OP Mainnet and Base has the same shape with a trade quota that one party can leave open for the next. Withdrawals keep working in all of these.

Limits of the design that the audits recorded and the team accepted (F-102, F-104, low, all networks). Any account can set the starting price of a new pool, or move the price of a pool with no liquidity in range at no cost, and core puts no price or amount bound on the first deposit. A provider is exposed only if its add carries no amount bound of its own. A donation goes to whoever holds liquidity in range at that instant, so liquidity added a moment earlier collects part of it. Only the donated amount is at stake.

The remaining six (F-006, F-014, F-054, F-055, F-100, F-101) record places where the source disagrees with its own comments, tests, test doubles or audit record, with no funds involved. The two new ones come from the audit walk. The Trail of Bits and Spearbit reports list a lock on `sync` and on fee collection as a fix, and the source has a check on the synced currency in their place, which skips `address(0)`. Where the native asset has an ERC-20 face, a native fee collection can therefore run between a `sync` of the face and its `settle`, and the account that holds the sync is the only one that pays. The OpenZeppelin report says `noDelegateCall` was added to all relevant external functions, and the source carries it on the four pool functions only. The reports do not say whether the guards were dropped on purpose later.

The other 62 findings need a party the threat model excludes: a token issuer, a chain operator, governance, or the author of a hook or token that the victims chose. They are listed near the end. Five issuer blocks are in force today and an ordinary account can neither set nor lift them.

- On Ethereum the issuers of yUSD, yETH and xSolvBTC have blocked the PoolManager address, and KITE and NTX are paused. About 1.67 million USD held for pools cannot leave (F-080).
- On Celo the GoodDollar owner block-listed the PoolManager on 11 September 2026, which holds about 60,000 USD of G$ (F-056).
- On Arbitrum One the owner of Pleasing USD burned 41,000 PUSD out of the PoolManager and then blacklisted it, which froze the PUSD side of eight pools (F-087).
- On Tempo a TIP-20 issuer has frozen one pooled token (F-065).
- On BNB Smart Chain the restrictor of HOLD has restricted the PoolManager address, which holds 140,775 HOLD worth about 15,600 USD (F-093).

The largest balances under issuer control that are not blocked today:

- Arc: 2.44 million USDC. One blocklist entry for the PoolManager would stop native USDC in both directions, its token face and the bridged WETH token, which reads the same list (F-108).
- Base: 5.32 million OUSD. The block list bound to it is administered by one account without code (F-107).
- Arbitrum One: 1.71 million USDG and 1.07 million Dinari USD+ (F-088). The USD+ index has already been lowered once after the deposit, through a signed storage write, and left the PoolManager 22.25 USD+ short of what the pools record.

One operator hazard is new. The MegaETH portal on L1 rejects a forced transaction above 1,000,000 gas, and any account can spend about 0.003 ETH to raise the storage price of a chosen position's slot above that. The position can then leave only through the sequencer, so a freeze still needs the sequencer to refuse the owner (F-105).

## What has been tested

One balance under two currency ids. Arc (native USDC with 18 decimals and an ERC-20 face with 6), Celo (CELO token), Polygon (`0x1010`), the legacy ETH token on Optimism and Zora, and stHYPE with wstHYPE on HyperEVM, which share one share balance. In each case `sync`, `settle` and `take` were walked across both faces in every order, twice by network researchers and once in a Python model of the settlement code. No order credits one deposit twice. Cycle 3 widened this to every address whose reported balance for the PoolManager could move with another ledger: a sweep of 248 pooled tokens across the 21 networks with zero and dust transfers from the PoolManager address. Wrapped native tokens exchange one for one, and wstETH, sAVAX and qiAVAX mint at their own rate with the caller debited in full. A native credit needs `settle` with value while no token is synced, `settle` rejects value while a token is synced (`src/PoolManager.sol:353-356`), and only one currency can be synced at a time. On Arc the 12 digit truncation gives at most 0.000001 USDC per round against a 20 gwei minimum base fee.

The PoolManager as caller of special addresses. `take` lets anyone make the PoolManager call any address with `transfer(address,uint256)` or with empty calldata, and `initialize` lets anyone name any address as a hook. Cycle 2 ran these call shapes from the PoolManager address against every precompile, predeploy and system contract the researchers could list: all 2,048 predeploy slots on each of the seven OP Stack chains, 122 special addresses on Polygon, BNB Smart Chain and Avalanche, and the full precompile sets of Tempo, Arc, Monad, MegaETH, HyperEVM and the Arbitrum family. Where a call succeeds, the caller owes the full amount, which is the payout-stranding group above.

Opcodes. A verifier deployed nothing and used `eth_call` with init code to run 60 arithmetic, shift, hash, transient storage, `MCOPY` and `PUSH0` vectors on each of the 21 networks, and a second verifier repeated it in cycle 2. All agree with Ethereum. Avalanche differs in one place: the C-Chain clears bit 248 of every storage slot index, so two indexes map to one slot (F-040). The PoolManager writes only to slots 0 to 6 and to hash outputs, so core is unaffected; a hook with hand-picked slots is not.

Transient storage across transactions. Read in client source for the OP Stack (op-revm, Base), Bor, BSC, AvalancheGo, Nitro, X Layer and Arc, and tested with `eth_simulateV1` on six OP Stack chains, Polygon and BNB Smart Chain. A second transaction always starts with an empty map. Batch transactions on Tempo and helper contracts on Arc share transient storage only among one sender's own calls.

A run inside a transaction with its own transient storage. `sync` needs no lock, so a network that let a contract start code under a fresh transient map while keeping its transfers could credit one payment twice. Cycle 3 read the client source of Tempo, Base, Nitro, MegaETH, Arc, Celo, Monad, Avalanche, Bor and BSC and ran two probes on all 21 endpoints: 519 calls to 173 special addresses left a transient slot intact, and every kind of revert restored transient storage together with ordinary state. No network offers such a run to an ordinary contract. The MegaETH keyless deploy sandbox has the right shape and starts only as the first frame of a transaction.

Call value without a balance debit. `settle` credits `msg.value`. On BNB Smart Chain a call from the system address `0xff…fe` carries its stated value and moves no balance (F-058), and nothing an ordinary account sends can run with that caller. Every other value-carrying path that was read debits the sender: OP Stack deposits, Nitro retryables, X Layer gasless transactions, Celo fee currency calls, HyperEVM system transactions, Tempo and Arc.

A chain without a native asset. On Tempo `CALLVALUE` is always 0 and value transfers are rejected, so the native branch of `settle` credits 0.

ERC-6909 operators and spenders. Cycle 5 read 30,347 approval logs on the 21 networks and found 765 addresses with a live operator right or allowance, with about 44,000 USD of priced claims behind them. None was found to let an arbitrary caller move another owner's claims. The Universal Router and the PositionManager hold approvals that do nothing, because their code has no `burn` or `transferFrom` call on the PoolManager. This rests on probes, traces and partial code reading, and several unverified contracts were followed only as far as their `unlock` call.

Forks that are scheduled. Jenner on BNB Smart Chain (8 December 2026) turns some token addresses into native code. Its issuer controls are recorded as F-076, no entry point lets an account without a role change another holder's balance, and a `balanceOf` that runs short of gas cannot return a false zero. Lagoon on OP Mainnet and Unichain (target 11 January 2027) adds interop predeploys. From their source, every call shape the PoolManager can send to them reverts, ETH that a relay forces into the PoolManager credits nobody, and a message relayed to the PoolManager acts only under the messenger's own address. On Base, a token built into the node (B20) takes the direct caller as the source of a transfer and rejects `DELEGATECALL`; that check protects about 5.32 million OUSD in the Base PoolManager and should be read again on every Base release.

The source itself. Ten readings and one audit walk so far: two line by line, a swap trace, a liquidity trace, an attempt to break the custody invariants with a search over call sequences, a check of every mask, shift, cast and `unchecked` block, and two replays of the repository's tests against `src/`. The replays reproduced every concrete vector and revert in the test files on a Python transcription, including 8,920 cases of the v3 comparison run from the bytecode fixture in `test/bin`, and a verifier matched the transcription against 370 quotes and 107 mined swaps on the live networks. Three independent Python transcriptions of the pool math stayed solvent across about 27,000 random operation sequences. The slot constants, error selectors, TickMath and BitMath tables were recomputed from the text. Cycle 6 took the places where the source is looser than its comments and test doubles: `sync` without a lock, `take` on an address without code, the functions without `noDelegateCall`, and the truncation of claim ids. None gives an ordinary account a gain or a freeze. It also listed which test guards each statement that moves funds. No test fails if the unsettled delta check at the end of `unlock` is removed, and with the isolation setting of current Foundry no test pins the lock close or the reset of the synced currency (F-089). The source has all three statements, so this is a gap in the tests.

The transaction filter on Robinhood Chain. The filter precompile holds hashes of single delayed inbox transactions, added by one filterer account a median of 10 blocks before the transaction runs. All 6,234 entries were read. None names the PoolManager or its periphery, and an ordinary account cannot cause an entry that matches other people's PoolManager transactions.

Celo and X Layer at their production tags. The Celo client takes its interpreter from a fork whose instruction files, journal and call frame are identical to the upstream version it names, and its one addition, the transfer precompile, rejects the PoolManager as caller. The X Layer client uses the published interpreter without an X Layer branch. The forks ahead of both (Karst, Lagoon, the second custom gas token design, Glamsterdam on L1) add nothing the PoolManager's call shapes can reach.

Complete currency lists. The Initialize history is now read in full on Base (15.3 million currencies in 15.5 million pools), BNB Smart Chain (78,255), World Chain (826) and X Layer (3,750). The sweeps for locked units, third-party balance changes and shared balances ran on every new currency with a balance on the last three and on 59,170 Base currencies that cover every code family. A census of 17.7 million transactions on the 21 networks flagged 3,752 rows where the PoolManager's balance fell without a matching pool amount, and each was read. What remains unexplained is covered by F-092 and by the tokens that move the balance themselves (F-086, F-094).

The audit reports. Cycle 7 took every finding of the OpenZeppelin, Trail of Bits, Certora, Spearbit and ABDK reports and of the hook permissions note, 113 entries in all, and named the statement in `src/` that closes it today and the network property that statement relies on. The critical OpenZeppelin finding on a native asset with an ERC-20 address is closed by the single synced currency and the value check in `settle` (`src/PoolManager.sol:285-286` and `356`), and a walk on all 21 deployed PoolManagers with the real face on Celo, Arc, Polygon, Optimism and Zora gave no double credit. No fix was found weaker than its report describes apart from the two absent guards above.

The PoolManager as inherited caller. The PoolManager calls code that anyone can supply in three places: the unlock callback, a hook, and the recipient of a native payout. From such a frame, cycle 7 called every natively implemented contract on HyperEVM, Monad and MegaETH with `DELEGATECALL`, `CALLCODE` and `STATICCALL` as well as `CALL`. None acts on the PoolManager's account or accepts a value that no balance backs. The Monad staking precompile rejects every call kind other than `CALL` before it reads the selector, the MegaETH interceptors test the target address, and on HyperEVM the bridge and CoreWriter logs are emitted under the calling contract's address, which HyperCore ignores. Earlier cycles had the same result for the built-in tokens of Base and Tempo, for Arc and for Celo.

The Base client source for built-in tokens. At the release mainnet runs, every balance write of a B20 token needs the owner as caller, an allowance, a token role, or the factory while it creates that same token, and `transfer` credits the named recipient or reverts. Nineteen calls from an ordinary account against OUSD and the policy registry with the PoolManager as holder all reverted or returned zero.

## What is open

The cycle 7 critic put these questions at the head of the queue.

- BNB Smart Chain on the first day after the Jenner fork (8 December 2026, 02:30 UTC). Five open leads wait for it: the call shapes of the PoolManager against a live built-in token, the inherited caller test, and one full round of `sync`, payment, `settle` and `take`.
- Transient storage of the most used hooks. A hook that keeps a value in transient storage without a pool id, or does not reset it, lets one party of a shared transaction affect the next, as the AGL hook does. The ten most used hooks on Base, Unichain, Ethereum, Robinhood Chain, Elysium, Arc and Monad have not been read for this.
- Exits on MegaETH without the sequencer. The verifier found that every removal of one vault contract used 1.17 to 3.24 million gas, more than a forced transaction may carry, which disputes an item the unit had cleared.
- Contracts that call `donate` from a balance they built up earlier on a function open to anyone, where liquidity placed around the call collects the donation.
- One table of every pooled token that an issuer can block, pause or remove at the PoolManager, with the value at stake. Four critics have asked for it and the entries are still spread over a dozen findings.

Three limits apply to everything above. First, the repository's own test suite has not been run: the PoolManager now builds here with the pinned compiler and matches its snapshot, but the tests need forge, which is not installed. The Yul optimizer bug published on 22 October 2026 for solc 0.8.26 was checked against this build in cycle 9 and does not act on it. Second, some node software could not be read: the HyperEVM validator is closed, the Nitro release that Arbitrum One and Robinhood Chain run is not public, and the Elysium build is unidentified. Third, the chain facts were read from the live official deployments, whose bytecode differs from this checkout, and the build of this checkout turned out to be the same bytes, so those facts describe this build as well.
