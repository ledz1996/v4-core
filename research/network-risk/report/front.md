# PoolManager network risk report

State on 2026-11-23: cycles 1 to 5 are merged. The ledger holds 87 findings, 598 checks that came out safe, and 364 open leads across the 21 networks. Eighty-eight agents have worked on it so far, about half of them as independent verifiers.

## The question

Can an ordinary account take funds that other users hold in this repository's PoolManager, or stop them from withdrawing, on any of the 21 networks where Uniswap v4 is officially deployed? The attacker has no role: no owner or fee controller key, no token admin key, no sequencer or validator power, no governance vote. The victims are liquidity providers, swappers and holders of ERC-6909 claims who use pools in the normal way.

## Answer so far

No critical or high finding exists for an ordinary attacker, and the planted weakness has not been identified. Five cycles have found no property of any of the 21 networks that lets an ordinary account reach funds in pools of ordinary tokens. Every finding that an ordinary account can trigger depends on a particular token or a particular third-party hook, and the amounts at stake today are small.

Cycle 5 asked four questions across all networks. Three produced findings: tokens that let a third party lock or remove what the PoolManager holds, a hook family whose fee ledger is shared between pools, and a gap in the PositionManager that turns a blocked token into a locked position. The fourth, whether any approved operator lets a stranger move other owners' claims, came out safe as far as it could be tested.

Thirty-six findings can be triggered by an ordinary account: 11 medium, 21 low and 4 that only record a mismatch between comments or tests and the source. They fall into eight groups.

Capture of balance growth (F-041, F-066, F-067 medium; F-060, F-081 low). Any account can call `sync`, cause the PoolManager's balance of a token to rise, and call `settle` to be credited the rise. For Ampleforth on Ethereum the public rebase is guarded by `msg.sender == tx.origin`, and an EIP-7702 delegated account passes that check while running code. On Polygon the capture is in regular use: since 22 May 2026 six accounts have wrapped each sLGNS rebase in `sync` and `settle` and minted the PoolManager's share as claims, 661 mints for 20,418.69 sLGNS, about 3,300 USD. A cycle 5 verifier found the same done with sSAC on BNB Smart Chain, five payout transactions, two of them traced at 2.538 sSAC each, where F-066 had recorded the path as unused. Smaller cases are Interest Protocol's USDI on Ethereum, Benqi rewards on qiAVAX on Avalanche, and TITHE on Robinhood Chain, where a public `claimFor` call pays 10,002.56 TITHE of holder rewards to the PoolManager. In every case the amount taken is growth that no pool, position or claim was credited with, and principal does not fall. A search of 53.3 million claim mint logs on the 21 networks found mints without a matching payment only on Polygon, in three ids that were already known.

Tokens that let a third party lock or remove the PoolManager's balance (F-078, F-079, F-085 medium; F-082 low). These reach funds that pools have recorded, which the growth captures above do not.

- Two QANX contracts and seventeen tokens from one generator count locked units in `balanceOf` and let any holder send locked units to another address. An account can take liquid units from the PoolManager, pay back in locked ones between `sync` and `settle`, and leave the pools with units that cannot be sent. A holder of locked QANX gains the liquid units. This works on Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain and Polygon. About 133 USD of current QANX and about 250 USD of generator tokens are exposed, and the 21.2 million old QANX in the Ethereum PoolManager belong to a contract its issuer abandoned in 2022.
- Old ADX and CDT on Ethereum let any holder grant revocable vesting tokens. An account can take X, grant X back to the PoolManager, settle, and then revoke the grant, which removes X with no debit. The verifier reproduced it with `eth_simulateV1`. The PoolManager holds 16.6 ADX and 310 CDT, without a market price.
- DeadToken on Base lets a holder of 1,000,000 DEAD claim the balance of any address that has been idle for 90 days. On 17 November 2026 an ordinary account borrowed that amount and took the PoolManager's whole balance, 14,899.78 DEAD worth about 4 USD, with no PoolManager call. The pools still record it.
- XAMP has a public negative rebase, and 37 unpriced tokens have burn or transfer functions without a caller check. The same call empties any other holder of these tokens.

The verifier rated the first two high as a class. The review files them as medium, in line with F-041, because the loss is limited to pools of the named tokens. A new deployment has the same exposure once someone creates a pool in such a token and others add liquidity to it. The search behind them ran 389,956 read-only trials over 1,235,609 pooled currencies and found such tokens on these three networks only.

A transfer cap that one account can use up (F-072, F-083, medium, Polygon). ViciCoin limits every address that its owner has not exempted to 6 VCNT of net outflow per UTC day, and the PoolManager is not exempt. An account that withdraws 6 VCNT at the start of a day makes every later VCNT withdrawal from every v4 pool revert until someone pays VCNT in. Cycle 5 added the consequence for positions. The PositionManager on all 21 networks rejects the `MINT_6909` action, so a removal built by the published SDK reverts as a whole when one currency cannot leave, and the other currency of the position stays locked with it. A hand-built exit that places the blocked side in a new one-sided position works. Of the 15 ViciCoin pools, 8 have a position held through the PositionManager, and the PoolManager holds about 206 USD of VCNT. On the other 20 networks the same lock needs a token issuer to block the PoolManager first.

A fee ledger shared between pools of one hook (F-084, medium, nine networks). The Klik fee hooks keep one pending fee amount per launched token and burn it in the currency of whichever pool of that token swaps next. They accept a pool of the token against any currency from any account. An account that opens a second pool against a currency of its choice can make the hook burn claims that back other tokens' fees. This has happened: an Ethereum transaction burned about 11 USD of claims in a tokenised stock currency, and three pools with liquidity now revert on every swap. Liquidity providers can still withdraw because the hooks have no liquidity callbacks. The hooks hold tens of USD per network. This is a fault in a third-party hook, and the PoolManager behaves as written. The hook census behind it probed 111 hooks on the 21 networks, and the other shared hooks it read, including FeeHookV3, Clanker and Doppler, reject a pool from an ordinary account or keep their ledgers per pool.

Yield that nobody can withdraw (F-012 stETH on Ethereum, medium; F-037 Aave aTokens on Arbitrum One and F-070 receipt tokens on thirteen networks, low). The balance grows outside pool accounting and core has no function that pays the growth out. Principal is untouched.

Payouts to a recipient that swallows them (F-033 medium; F-021, F-047, F-048, F-049 low). On every network family some address accepts a payout from the PoolManager and books it to the PoolManager itself, or holds it: HyperCore bridge addresses on HyperEVM, `L2ToL1MessagePasser` and the ERC-4337 EntryPoints on the OP Stack chains, withdrawal predeploys and fee vaults on Celo and X Layer, precompiles and custody addresses on Arc and Tempo, and Tempo accounts whose receive policy rejects the PoolManager. The caller is debited in full each time, so the only loser is the caller, or the user of a router that passes a recipient through unchecked.

Currency ids that take credit and cannot pay out (F-008, F-077, F-050, F-025, low). Polygon's system token at `0x1010`, the USDC implementation address on Arc, and the Celo predeploy that names itself WETH and wraps CELO. Only someone who pays into such an id loses. One empty pool with `0x1010` as a currency now exists on Polygon.

Delay without loss (F-038, F-039, F-042, F-044, F-062, F-063, F-064, F-071, low). One cheap transaction on MegaETH uses a block's whole budget for new state. A HyperEVM small block fits about 200 tick crossings in one swap. A HyperCore transfer runs a linked contract ahead of every signed transaction in the block. Any account can fill one tick to the liquidity cap for dust, on every network. On Tempo a first crossing of a tick with a dust position costs about 505,000 gas. In a transaction that several users share, a `sync` left by an earlier user makes a later user's native payment revert if that user's router sends value without its own `sync`. Withdrawals keep working in all of these.

The remaining four (F-006, F-014, F-054, F-055) record places where the source disagrees with its own comments, tests or test doubles, with no funds involved.

The other 51 findings need a party the threat model excludes: a token issuer, a chain operator, governance, or the author of a hook or token that the victims chose. They are listed near the end. Four issuer blocks are in force today and an ordinary account can neither set nor lift them.

- On Ethereum the issuers of yUSD, yETH and xSolvBTC have blocked the PoolManager address, and KITE and NTX are paused. About 1.67 million USD held for pools cannot leave (F-080).
- On Celo the GoodDollar owner block-listed the PoolManager on 11 September 2026, which holds about 60,000 USD of G$ (F-056).
- On Arbitrum One the owner of Pleasing USD burned 41,000 PUSD out of the PoolManager and then blacklisted it, which froze the PUSD side of eight pools (F-087).
- On Tempo a TIP-20 issuer has frozen one pooled token (F-065).

## What has been tested

One balance under two currency ids. Arc (native USDC with 18 decimals and an ERC-20 face with 6), Celo (CELO token), Polygon (`0x1010`), the legacy ETH token on Optimism and Zora, and stHYPE with wstHYPE on HyperEVM, which share one share balance. In each case `sync`, `settle` and `take` were walked across both faces in every order, twice by network researchers and once in a Python model of the settlement code. No order credits one deposit twice. Cycle 3 widened this to every address whose reported balance for the PoolManager could move with another ledger: a sweep of 248 pooled tokens across the 21 networks with zero and dust transfers from the PoolManager address. Wrapped native tokens exchange one for one, and wstETH, sAVAX and qiAVAX mint at their own rate with the caller debited in full. A native credit needs `settle` with value while no token is synced, `settle` rejects value while a token is synced (`src/PoolManager.sol:353-356`), and only one currency can be synced at a time. On Arc the 12 digit truncation gives at most 0.000001 USDC per round against a 20 gwei minimum base fee.

The PoolManager as caller of special addresses. `take` lets anyone make the PoolManager call any address with `transfer(address,uint256)` or with empty calldata, and `initialize` lets anyone name any address as a hook. Cycle 2 ran these call shapes from the PoolManager address against every precompile, predeploy and system contract the researchers could list: all 2,048 predeploy slots on each of the seven OP Stack chains, 122 special addresses on Polygon, BNB Smart Chain and Avalanche, and the full precompile sets of Tempo, Arc, Monad, MegaETH, HyperEVM and the Arbitrum family. Where a call succeeds, the caller owes the full amount, which is the payout-stranding group above.

Opcodes. A verifier deployed nothing and used `eth_call` with init code to run 60 arithmetic, shift, hash, transient storage, `MCOPY` and `PUSH0` vectors on each of the 21 networks, and a second verifier repeated it in cycle 2. All agree with Ethereum. Avalanche differs in one place: the C-Chain clears bit 248 of every storage slot index, so two indexes map to one slot (F-040). The PoolManager writes only to slots 0 to 6 and to hash outputs, so core is unaffected; a hook with hand-picked slots is not.

Transient storage across transactions. Read in client source for the OP Stack (op-revm, Base), Bor, BSC, AvalancheGo, Nitro, X Layer and Arc, and tested with `eth_simulateV1` on six OP Stack chains, Polygon and BNB Smart Chain. A second transaction always starts with an empty map. Batch transactions on Tempo and helper contracts on Arc share transient storage only among one sender's own calls.

A run inside a transaction with its own transient storage. `sync` needs no lock, so a network that let a contract start code under a fresh transient map while keeping its transfers could credit one payment twice. Cycle 3 read the client source of Tempo, Base, Nitro, MegaETH, Arc, Celo, Monad, Avalanche, Bor and BSC and ran two probes on all 21 endpoints: 519 calls to 173 special addresses left a transient slot intact, and every kind of revert restored transient storage together with ordinary state. No network offers such a run to an ordinary contract. The MegaETH keyless deploy sandbox has the right shape and starts only as the first frame of a transaction.

Call value without a balance debit. `settle` credits `msg.value`. On BNB Smart Chain a call from the system address `0xff…fe` carries its stated value and moves no balance (F-058), and nothing an ordinary account sends can run with that caller. Every other value-carrying path that was read debits the sender: OP Stack deposits, Nitro retryables, X Layer gasless transactions, Celo fee currency calls, HyperEVM system transactions, Tempo and Arc.

A chain without a native asset. On Tempo `CALLVALUE` is always 0 and value transfers are rejected, so the native branch of `settle` credits 0.

ERC-6909 operators and spenders. Cycle 5 read 30,347 approval logs on the 21 networks and found 765 addresses with a live operator right or allowance, with about 44,000 USD of priced claims behind them. None was found to let an arbitrary caller move another owner's claims. The Universal Router and the PositionManager hold approvals that do nothing, because their code has no `burn` or `transferFrom` call on the PoolManager. This rests on probes, traces and partial code reading, and several unverified contracts were followed only as far as their `unlock` call.

Forks that are scheduled. Jenner on BNB Smart Chain (8 December 2026) turns some token addresses into native code. Its issuer controls are recorded as F-076, no entry point lets an account without a role change another holder's balance, and a `balanceOf` that runs short of gas cannot return a false zero. Lagoon on OP Mainnet and Unichain (target 11 January 2027) adds interop predeploys. From their source, every call shape the PoolManager can send to them reverts, ETH that a relay forces into the PoolManager credits nobody, and a message relayed to the PoolManager acts only under the messenger's own address. On Base, a token built into the node (B20) takes the direct caller as the source of a transfer and rejects `DELEGATECALL`; that check protects about 5.32 million OUSD in the Base PoolManager and should be read again on every Base release.

The source itself. Eight readings so far: two line by line, a swap trace, a liquidity trace, an attempt to break the custody invariants with a search over call sequences, a check of every mask, shift, cast and `unchecked` block, and two replays of the repository's tests against `src/`. The replays reproduced every concrete vector and revert in the test files on a Python transcription, including 8,920 cases of the v3 comparison run from the bytecode fixture in `test/bin`, and a verifier matched the transcription against 370 quotes and 107 mined swaps on the live networks. Three independent Python transcriptions of the pool math stayed solvent across about 27,000 random operation sequences. The slot constants, error selectors, TickMath and BitMath tables were recomputed from the text.

## What is open

The cycle 5 critic put these questions at the head of the queue.

- Hooks ranked by what they hold. For the hooks with the largest claim and token balances on each network: is the ledger that decides a payout stored per pool, and can an ordinary account reach it with a currency of its choice, as with the Klik hooks? Arc has 194,252 hook addresses that nobody has grouped yet.
- The full currency lists of Base, BNB Smart Chain, World Chain and X Layer. The sweeps for locked units, revocable grants and third-party balance changes covered only part of these four networks.
- Every pooled token worth more than 1,000 USD at a PoolManager, tested with each of its functions that takes an address, at the current time and with the clock moved forward, so that inactivity sweeps such as DeadToken's show up before they are due.
- The verified source of valuable pooled tokens with lock, vesting, cooldown or escrow functions, among them sUSDe, sENA, syrupUSDC and ondo on Ethereum.
- A second pass over the transfer census without labels, because two rows that the first pass called spoof tokens were real payouts.
- The transaction filter on Robinhood Chain: what an entry matches and whether an ordinary account can cause one that covers the PoolManager.

No unit has read `src/` since cycle 3, and two code leads are still open: the places where the source is looser than its comments and test doubles, and which test guards each line that moves funds. Cycle 6 takes both.

Three limits apply to everything above. No compiler is installed here, so nothing was built, and two questions depend on that: whether the build matches the size and creation code hash recorded in `snapshots/PoolManagerTest.json`, and whether a Yul optimizer bug published on 22 October 2026, which the Solidity bug list marks as affecting the pinned solc 0.8.26, touches the optimized output. Second, some node software could not be read: the HyperEVM validator is closed, the Nitro release that Arbitrum One and Robinhood Chain run is not public, and the Elysium build is unidentified. Third, the chain facts were read from the live official deployments, whose bytecode differs from this checkout, so the token and hook findings describe what the new deployment will meet and do not test a defect in this build.
